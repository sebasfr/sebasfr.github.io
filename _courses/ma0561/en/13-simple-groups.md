## Simple Groups

### Definition (Simple group)

Let $$G$$ be a non-trivial group. We say that $$G$$ is simple if it has no proper normal subgroups, i.e., if $$H \triangleleft G$$, then $$H = \{ 1_{G} \}$$ or $$H=G$$.

### Theorem (Simple abelian groups are $$\mathbb{Z}_{p}$$)

Let $$G$$ be an abelian group. Then $$G$$ is simple if and only if $$G=\mathbb{Z}_{p}$$ with $$p$$ prime.

***Proof:*** ($$\impliedby$$): This direction is straightforward, since it suffices to apply Lagrange to note that $$G$$ has no proper subgroups.
($$\implies$$): Since $$G$$ is abelian, every subgroup is normal. Moreover, since $$G$$ is simple, $$G \neq \{ 1_{G} \}$$. Then there exists $$x \in G$$ such that $$(x \neq 1_{G})$$. We have that

$$
\langle x \rangle \triangleleft  G \implies \langle x \rangle = G \implies G \text{ is cyclic} \implies G \cong \mathbb{Z} \quad \lor \quad G \cong   \mathbb{Z}_{n}, \text{ for some } n \in \mathbb{N}.
$$

Since $$G$$ is simple, $$G \not\cong \mathbb{Z}$$, so $$G \cong \mathbb{Z}_{n}$$ for some $$n \in  \mathbb{N}$$. Moreover, since $$G$$ is simple, $$n$$ is prime, from which we conclude the result.

### Reminder (Sign and alternating group)

If $$\sigma \in S_{n}$$ is such that $$\sigma = \beta_{1} \cdots \beta_{t}$$ is its decomposition into disjoint cycles of $$\sigma$$, then $$\operatorname{sgn}( \sigma ) := (-1)^{n-t}$$. If $$\operatorname{sgn}( \sigma ) = 1$$ we say that $$\sigma$$ is even and $$A_{n} = \{ \sigma \in S_{n}, \sigma \text{ is even} \}.$$

### Exercises (On $$3$$-cycles)

1. $$A_{n}$$ is generated by the 3-cycles if $$n\geq {5}$$.
2. In $$S_{5}$$, all 3-cycles are conjugate.
3. In $$A_{5}$$, all 3-cycles are conjugate.

### Theorem ($$A_{5}$$ is simple)

$$A_{5}$$ is simple

***Proof:*** Let $$H \neq \{ \mathrm{id}_{S_{5}} \}$$ be such that $$H \triangleleft A_{5}$$. By the previous exercises, it suffices to show that $$H$$ contains a 3-cycle, since if $$\sigma \in H$$ is a 3-cycle, then $$\sigma^{A_{5}} \subseteq H$$ because $$H \triangleleft A_{5}$$, hence $$\langle \sigma^{A_{5}}  \rangle = \{ \tau: \tau \text{ is a 3-cycle}  \} \subseteq H$$ and therefore $$H = A_{5}$$.

How many possible configurations are there for the decomposition into disjoint cycles?

1. $$\sigma$$ decomposes into 5 1-cycles, and therefore $$\sigma \in A_{5}$$.
2. $$\sigma$$ decomposes into one 2-cycle and 3 1-cycles, and therefore $$\sigma \not\in A_{5}$$.
3. $$\sigma$$ decomposes into one 3-cycle and 2 1-cycles, and therefore $$\sigma \in A_{5}$$.
4. $$\sigma$$ decomposes into one 4-cycle and 1 1-cycle, and therefore $$\sigma \not\in A_{5}$$.
5. $$\sigma$$ decomposes into one 5-cycle, and therefore $$\sigma \in A_{5}$$.
6. $$\sigma$$ decomposes into two 2-cycles and one 1-cycle, and therefore $$\sigma \in A_{5}$$.
7. $$\sigma$$ decomposes into one 3-cycle and one 2-cycle, and therefore $$\sigma \not\in A_{5}$$.

Hence, if $$\sigma \in H \setminus \{ 1_{G} \}$$, its factorisation is of type 3, 5 or 6. If $$\sigma$$ is of type 3, then it contains a 3-cycle and therefore the result holds.
If $$\sigma$$ is of type 5, suppose without loss of generality that $$\sigma = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5\end{pmatrix}$$. Let $$\alpha = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3\end{pmatrix} \in A_{5}$$. Hence,

$$
w = \begin{pmatrix}
2 & 3 & 1 & 4 & 5
\end{pmatrix} =\alpha \sigma \alpha^{-1} \in H, \text{ since }H \triangleleft A_{5} \implies w \sigma ^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 2 & 4
\end{pmatrix} \in H.
$$

If $$\sigma$$ is of type 6, suppose without loss of generality that $$\sigma = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3 & 4\end{pmatrix}$$. Let $$\beta = \begin{pmatrix}3 & 4 & 5\end{pmatrix} \in A_{5}$$. Note that $$\delta :=\beta \sigma \beta ^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}4 & 5\end{pmatrix} \in H$$. Finally, $$\sigma \cdot \delta = \begin{pmatrix}3 & 4 & 5\end{pmatrix} \in H$$.
Conclude that $$A_{5}$$ is simple.

### Theorem ($$A_{6}$$ is simple)

$$A_{6}$$ is simple

***Proof:*** Let $$H\neq \{ \mathrm{id}_{S_{6}} \}$$ be such that $$H \triangleleft A_{6}$$. Suppose for contradiction that if $$\sigma \in H \setminus \{ \mathrm{id}_{S_{6}} \}$$, then $$\sigma$$ moves every element of $$\{ 1,2,\dots,6 \}$$. How many possible configurations are there for the decomposition into disjoint cycles?

1. $$\sigma$$ decomposes into three 2-cycles, and therefore $$\sigma \not\in A_{6}$$.
2. $$\sigma$$ decomposes into one 2-cycle and one 4-cycle, and therefore $$\sigma \in A_{6}$$.
3. $$\sigma$$ decomposes into two 3-cycles, and therefore $$\sigma \in A_{6}$$.

If $$\sigma$$ is of type 2, consider $$\sigma = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3 & 4 & 5 & 6\end{pmatrix}$$. Since $$H$$ is a subgroup, if $$\sigma \in H$$, then $$\sigma^{2} \in H$$. But $$\sigma^{2}(1) = 1$$, a contradiction.
On the other hand, if $$\sigma$$ is of type 3, consider $$\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}4 & 5 & 6\end{pmatrix}$$. Hence we have that $$\sigma ^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}3 & 2 & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}6 & 5 & 4\end{pmatrix}$$. Let $$\gamma = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 3 & 4\end{pmatrix}$$. Since $$H$$ is a subgroup, we have that $$\sigma\gamma \sigma ^{-1} \gamma ^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 5 & 3 & 2 & 4\end{pmatrix} \in H$$, a contradiction.
Conclude that there exist $$\sigma \in H$$ and $$i \in \{ 1,\dots,6 \}$$ such that $$\sigma(i) = i$$. Let $$F = \{ \sigma \in A_{6}: \sigma(i)=i \}$$. Note that $$F \neq \{ 1_{S_{6}} \}$$ and $$F \cap H \neq \{ 1_{S_{6}} \}$$. It is easy to see that $$F \cong A_{5}$$. Finally, since $$F \cap H \triangleleft F \cong A_{5}$$ and $$A_{5}$$ is simple, we have that $$F \cap H = F$$, hence $$F \subseteq H$$. Conclude that $$A_{6}$$ is simple because it contains a 3-cycle.

### Theorem ($$A_{n}$$ is simple for $$n \geq 5$$)

$$A_{n}$$ is simple for $$n \geq5$$

***Proof:*** Consider the case $$n>6$$. Let $$H\neq \{ \mathrm{id}_{S_{n}} \}$$ be such that $$H \triangleleft A_{n}$$ Let $$\sigma \in H \setminus \{ \mathrm{id}_{Sn} \}$$. Then there exists $$i \in \{ 1,\dots,n \}$$ such that $$\sigma(i) = j \neq i$$. Let $$\alpha$$ be a 3-cycle such that $$\alpha(i)=i$$ and $$\alpha(j)\neq j$$. Note that

$$
\alpha(\sigma(i)) = \alpha(j) \neq  j  \quad \land  \quad\sigma(\alpha(i)) = \sigma(i) =j \implies \alpha \sigma \neq \sigma\alpha \implies \alpha\sigma\alpha ^{-1}\sigma ^{-1} \in H \setminus \{1_{S+n}\}.
$$

We know that conjugation of permutations does not change the cycle structure. Hence $$\sigma \alpha ^{-1} \sigma ^{-1}$$ is a 3-cycle and $$\gamma = \alpha\sigma\alpha ^{-1}\sigma ^{-1} \in H$$ is a product of two 3-cycles and therefore moves at most 6 elements. Let $$i_{1},\dots i_{k}$$, with $$k \leq 6$$, be the elements that $$\gamma$$ moves. Now, let $$F:=\{ \sigma \in A_{n}: \sigma(\ell) = \ell  \quad \forall \ell \not\in \{ i_{1},\dots,i_{k} \}\}$$. It is easy to see that $$F \cong S_{6}$$. and $$F \cap H \neq \{ 1_{S_{n}} \}$$. Thus, $$F \cap H \triangleleft F \cong A_{6}$$, so $$F \cap H = F$$ and therefore $$F \subseteq H$$. In particular, $$\begin{pmatrix}i_{1} & i_{2} & i_{3}\end{pmatrix} \in F \subseteq H$$, from which we conclude that it is simple because it contains a $$3-$$cycle..
