## Definition and examples

### Definition (Left, right and two-sided $$R$$-ideal)

Let $$R$$ be a ring and $$I \subseteq R$$ with $$I \neq \emptyset$$. We say that $$I$$ is a *left $$R$$-ideal* (respectively, a *right ideal*) if:

1. $$\forall x, y \in I\ (x + y \in I)$$;
2. $$\forall r \in R\ \forall x \in I\ (r \cdot x \in I)$$ (respectively, $$x \cdot r \in I$$); this property is called *absorption*.

If $$I$$ is both a right and a left $$R$$-ideal, we say that $$I$$ is an *$$R$$-ideal*.

### Note (First properties of ideals)

Let $$I$$ be an $$R$$-ideal (left or right).

1. $$(I, +)$$ is a group. Note that $$0 \in I$$: indeed, since $$0 \in R$$ and the proposition on arithmetic rules gives $$0 \cdot x = 0$$ for every $$x \in R$$, absorption applied to any $$x \in I$$ shows that $$0 = 0 \cdot x \in I$$.
2. Let $$x \in I$$. Then $$(-1) \cdot x = -x \in I$$.
3. If $$1 \in I$$, then $$I = R$$, since if $$a \in R$$, absorption gives $$a \cdot 1 = a \in I$$.

### Example (Trivial ideals and the ideals of a field)

The first examples of ideals:

1. If $$R$$ is a ring, $$R$$ and $$\{0\}$$ are ideals; they are called the *trivial ideals*.
2. Let $$F$$ be a field and $$I$$ an ideal in $$F$$ such that $$I \neq \{0\}$$. Let $$a \in I$$ with $$a \neq 0$$. Then

    $$
    a^{-1} \in F \implies a^{-1} \cdot a \in I \implies 1 \in I \implies I = F.
    $$

    We conclude that a field possesses only the two trivial ideals.

### Example (Ideals in polynomial rings)

Let $$R$$ be a commutative ring.

1. Let $$I \subseteq R[x]$$ be such that

    $$
    I = \{ p \in R[x] :\ \text{the constant term of } p \text{ is zero} \}.
    $$

    Then $$I$$ is an ideal.
2. If $$I$$ is an ideal of $$R$$, define

    $$
    I[x] = \{ p \in R[x] :\ p(x) \text{ has all its coefficients in } I \}.
    $$

    Then $$I[x]$$ is an ideal in $$R[x]$$.

### Example (Ideals in $$\mathbb{Z}$$ and in $$\mathbb{Z}_{n}$$)

Two families of ideals in the rings of integers:

1. $$n\mathbb{Z}$$ is an ideal of $$\mathbb{Z}$$.
2. Let $$n > 1$$ and consider $$\mathbb{Z}_{n}$$. Let $$d \in \mathbb{Z}_{n}$$ be such that $$d \mid n$$. Then $$\langle d \rangle$$ (the additive group generated by $$d$$) is an ideal.

### Example (A left ideal that is not a right ideal)

Let $$R = M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})$$ and define

$$
I := \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} a & 0 \\ b & 0 \end{pmatrix} :\ a, b \in \mathbb{R} \right\}.
$$

Note that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{pmatrix} x & y \\ z & w \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a & 0 \\ b & 0 \end{pmatrix} &= \begin{pmatrix} xa + yb & 0 \\ za + wb & 0 \end{pmatrix} \in I, \\
\begin{pmatrix} a & 0 \\ b & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x & y \\ z & w \end{pmatrix} &= \begin{pmatrix} ax & ay \\ bx & by \end{pmatrix} \notin I \ \text{in general},
\end{aligned}
$$

so $$I$$ is a left ideal but not a right ideal.

### Definition (Nilpotent element and the nilradical)

If $$R$$ is a ring, $$a \in R$$ is *nilpotent* if there exists $$n \in \mathbb{N}$$ such that $$a^{n} = 0$$. The least such $$n$$ is the *index of nilpotency* of $$a$$. Moreover,

$$
\operatorname{Nil}(R) = \{ x \in R :\ x \text{ is nilpotent} \}
$$

is an ideal, called the *nilradical* of $$R$$ (exercise).

## Operations with ideals and the generated ideal

### Definition (Sum, intersection and product of ideals)

Let $$R$$ be a commutative ring and let $$I, J$$ be ideals. Define:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I + J &:= \{ a + b :\ a \in I,\ b \in J \}, \\
I \cap J &:= \{ x :\ x \in I \wedge x \in J \}, \\
I \cdot J &:= \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} \cdot b_{i} :\ a_{i} \in I,\ b_{i} \in J,\ n \in \mathbb{N} \right\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

### Theorem (The operations on ideals produce ideals)

$$I + J$$, $$I \cap J$$ and $$I \cdot J$$ are ideals.

***Proof:*** Exercise.

### Corollary (The intersection of ideals is an ideal)

The intersection of any family of ideals of $$R$$ is an ideal of $$R$$.

***Proof:*** Let $$\{I_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha \in \Delta}$$ be a family of ideals and $$I = \bigcap_{\alpha \in \Delta} I_{\alpha}$$. Since $$0 \in I_{\alpha}$$ for every $$\alpha$$, we have that $$0 \in I$$, and in particular $$I \neq \emptyset$$. If $$x, y \in I$$, then $$x, y \in I_{\alpha}$$ for every $$\alpha$$, and since each $$I_{\alpha}$$ is closed under sums, $$x + y \in I_{\alpha}$$ for every $$\alpha$$, that is, $$x + y \in I$$. Finally, if $$r \in R$$ and $$x \in I$$, absorption in each $$I_{\alpha}$$ gives $$r x \in I_{\alpha}$$ for every $$\alpha$$, so that $$r x \in I$$ (and analogously $$x r \in I$$). We conclude that $$I$$ is an ideal.

### Definition (Generated, finitely generated and principal ideal)

Let $$R$$ be a commutative ring and $$U \subseteq R$$.

1. $$(U) := \bigcap_{U \subseteq I,\ I \text{ ideal}} I$$ is the *ideal generated* by $$U$$. Note that $$(U)$$ is an ideal, since the intersection of ideals is an ideal. If $$U$$ is finite and $$U = \{u_{1}, \dots, u_{n}\}$$, we write $$(u_{1}, \dots, u_{n}) = (U)$$.
2. We say that an ideal $$I$$ is *finitely generated* if there exists a finite $$U \subseteq R$$ such that $$I = (U)$$.
3. We say that $$I$$ is a *principal ideal* if there exists $$a \in R$$ such that $$I = (a)$$. Note that in this case $$I = (a) = \{ r \cdot a :\ r \in R \}$$ (exercise).

### Theorem (Characterisation of the generated ideal as combinations)

Let $$R$$ be a commutative ring and $$\emptyset \neq U \subseteq R$$. Then

$$
(U) = \underbrace{\left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} u_{i} :\ n \in \mathbb{N},\ a_{i} \in R,\ u_{i} \in U \right\}}_{J}.
$$

***Proof:*** Let us first see that $$J$$ is an ideal: the sum of two combinations of the form $$\sum a_{i} u_{i}$$ is again a combination of that type, and if $$r \in R$$, then $$r \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} u_{i} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (r a_{i}) u_{i} \in J$$, which gives absorption. Moreover $$U \subseteq J$$, since $$u = 1 \cdot u$$ for each $$u \in U$$. Then $$J$$ is one of the ideals we intersect in order to define $$(U)$$, and therefore $$(U) \subseteq J$$.

Conversely, let $$w \in J$$. Then there exist $$n \in \mathbb{N}$$, $$a_{i} \in R$$ and $$u_{i} \in U$$ such that $$w = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} u_{i}$$. Let $$I$$ be any ideal such that $$U \subseteq I$$. Then, by absorption, $$a_{i} u_{i} \in I$$ for every $$i$$, and by closure of the sum $$\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} u_{i} \in I$$, so that $$w \in I$$. Since $$I$$ is arbitrary, we have that

$$
w \in \bigcap_{U \subseteq I,\ I \text{ ideal}} I = (U),
$$

that is, $$J \subseteq (U)$$. We conclude the equality.

### Example (Computations with generated ideals)

Let $$R$$ be a commutative ring.

1. $$(0) = \{0\}$$ and $$(1) = R$$. If $$u \in R^{\times}$$, then $$(u) = R$$. In general, if $$u_{1}, \dots, u_{n} \in I$$ with $$I$$ an ideal, then $$(u_{1}, \dots, u_{n}) \subseteq I$$.
2. If $$R = \mathbb{Z}$$, then $$(6, 15) = (3)$$: since $$6 = 3 \cdot 2$$ and $$15 = 3 \cdot 5$$, we have that $$6, 15 \in (3)$$ and therefore $$(6, 15) \subseteq (3)$$. On the other hand, $$3 = 6 \cdot (-2) + 15$$, so $$3 \in (6, 15)$$ and therefore $$(3) \subseteq (6, 15)$$.
3. Consider the polynomial ring $$R[x]$$. Note that

    $$
    (x) = \{ x \cdot p(x) :\ p(x) \in R[x] \} = \{ r(x) :\ \text{the constant coefficient of } r(x) \text{ is zero} \}.
    $$

### Definition (Principal ideal ring and PID)

We collect two notions:

1. A *principal ideal ring* is a ring in which every ideal is principal.
2. A *principal ideal domain* (PID) is a principal ideal ring that is an integral domain.

### Example ($$\mathbb{Z}[x]$$ is not a principal ideal ring)

Let $$R = \mathbb{Z}[x]$$. One can prove that $$(2, x)$$ is not principal (exercise). Then $$\mathbb{Z}[x]$$ is not a principal ideal ring.

### Theorem ($$\mathbb{Z}$$ is a PID, and $$K[x]$$ is a PID if $$K$$ is a field)

$$\mathbb{Z}$$ is a principal ideal domain. If $$K$$ is a field, then $$K[x]$$ is a principal ideal domain.

***Proof:*** We already know that $$\mathbb{Z}$$ is an integral domain. Clearly $$\{0\} = (0)$$ is principal. Let $$I \neq \{0\}$$ be an ideal of $$\mathbb{Z}$$. Then there exists $$a \in I$$ with $$a \neq 0$$; since $$-a \in I$$, there is at least one element of $$\mathbb{Z}^{+}$$ in $$I$$. Let $$b \in I$$ be the smallest positive element of $$I$$. We shall prove that $$I = (b)$$. Clearly $$(b) \subseteq I$$, by absorption. Let $$a \in I$$. By the division algorithm in $$\mathbb{Z}$$, there exist $$q, r \in \mathbb{Z}$$ such that

$$
a = q b + r, \quad \text{with } 0 \leq r < b.
$$

Then $$a - q b = r \in I$$, since $$a \in I$$ and $$q b \in I$$. By the minimality of $$b$$, necessarily $$r = 0$$; then $$a = q b$$, so that $$a \in (b)$$ and therefore $$I \subseteq (b)$$. We conclude that $$I = (b)$$.

The proof that $$K[x]$$ is a PID if $$K$$ is a field is left as an exercise.

### Definition (Relatively prime ideals)

Let $$R$$ be a commutative ring and let $$I, J$$ be ideals. We say that $$I$$ and $$J$$ are *relatively prime* if $$I + J = R$$.

### Proposition (Properties of the sum and the product of ideals)

Let $$R$$ be a commutative ring and let $$I, J, K$$ be ideals. Then:

1. $$I + J = J + I$$ and $$I J = J I$$;
2. $$I + (J + K) = (I + J) + K$$ and $$(I J) K = I (J K)$$;
3. $$I (J + K) = I J + I K$$;
4. $$(0) + I = I$$, $$R \cdot I = I$$ and $$I + R = R$$;
5. $$(0) \cdot I = (0)$$;
6. $$I J \subseteq I \cap J$$.

***Proof:*** For (1): $$I + J = J + I$$ is immediate from the commutativity of the sum in $$R$$, and $$I J = J I$$ because each generator satisfies $$a_{i} b_{i} = b_{i} a_{i}$$, as $$R$$ is commutative.

For (2): both sides of $$I + (J + K) = (I + J) + K$$ are $$\{ a + b + c :\ a \in I,\ b \in J,\ c \in K \}$$, by the associativity of the sum. For the product, note that every element of $$(I J) K$$ is a finite sum $$\sum_{i} w_{i} c_{i}$$ with $$w_{i} \in I J$$ and $$c_{i} \in K$$; writing each $$w_{i}$$ as a finite sum of products $$a b$$, and distributing, $$(I J) K$$ is exactly the set of finite sums of triple products $$(a b) c$$ with $$a \in I$$, $$b \in J$$, $$c \in K$$. The same argument describes $$I (J K)$$ as the finite sums of products $$a (b c)$$, and the associativity of $$R$$ gives the equality.

For (3): “$$\subseteq$$” because each generator satisfies $$a (b + c) = a b + a c \in I J + I K$$, and the finite sums of these lie in the ideal $$I J + I K$$. “$$\supseteq$$” because $$I J \subseteq I (J + K)$$ (every $$b \in J$$ is written as $$b = b + 0 \in J + K$$) and $$I K \subseteq I (J + K)$$, and the ideal $$I (J + K)$$ is closed under sums.

For (4): $$(0) + I = I$$ since $$0 + a = a$$; $$R \cdot I = I$$ since “$$\subseteq$$” is absorption and “$$\supseteq$$” follows from $$a = 1 \cdot a$$; and $$I + R = R$$ since every $$a \in R$$ is written as $$a = 0 + a$$.

For (5): each generator of $$(0) \cdot I$$ is of the form $$0 \cdot b = 0$$, so $$(0) \cdot I = \{0\} = (0)$$.

For (6): each generator $$a b$$ with $$a \in I$$, $$b \in J$$ belongs to $$I$$ (absorption of $$I$$, with $$b \in R$$) and to $$J$$ (absorption of $$J$$, with $$a \in R$$); since $$I \cap J$$ is an ideal, the finite sums of such products lie in $$I \cap J$$, that is, $$I J \subseteq I \cap J$$.

## Maximal ideals and Krull's theorem

### Definition (Maximal ideal and the maximal spectrum)

Let $$R$$ be a commutative ring. An ideal $$\mathfrak{m} \neq R$$ is *maximal* if whenever $$J$$ is an ideal such that $$\mathfrak{m} \subseteq J$$, then $$J = R$$ or $$J = \mathfrak{m}$$. We denote by $$\operatorname{mSpec}(R)$$ the set of maximal ideals of $$R$$.

### Example (Known maximal spectra)

We compute $$\operatorname{mSpec}$$ in the basic examples:

1. If $$R = \{0\}$$, then $$\operatorname{mSpec}(R) = \emptyset$$.
2. If $$K$$ is a field, then $$\operatorname{mSpec}(K) = \{ \{0\} \}$$.
3. Let $$R = \mathbb{Z}_{4}$$. The ideals are $$(0)$$, $$(2) = \{0, 2\}$$ and $$(1) = \mathbb{Z}_{4} = (3)$$. Note that $$\operatorname{mSpec}(\mathbb{Z}_{4}) = \{(2)\}$$.
4. In $$\mathbb{Z}$$ all the ideals are of the form $$n\mathbb{Z}$$ with $$n \in \mathbb{N}$$. Show that $$n\mathbb{Z}$$ is maximal if and only if $$n$$ is prime.

### Axiom (Zorn's lemma)

Let $$(X, \leq)$$ be a partially ordered set. If

1. $$X \neq \emptyset$$, and
2. every totally ordered subset of $$X$$ possesses an upper bound in $$X$$,

then $$X$$ possesses a maximal element.

### Theorem (Krull: every proper ideal is contained in a maximal one)

Let $$R \neq \{0\}$$ be a commutative ring and let $$I$$ be a proper ideal. Then there exists a maximal ideal $$\mathfrak{m}$$ such that $$I \subseteq \mathfrak{m}$$.

***Proof:*** Let

$$
\Omega := \{ J :\ J \text{ ideal},\ J \neq R,\ I \subseteq J \},
$$

which is a set partially ordered by inclusion. Note that $$\Omega \neq \emptyset$$, since $$I \in \Omega$$. In order to apply Zorn's lemma, let $$(C_{\alpha})_{\alpha \in \Delta}$$ be a chain in $$\Omega$$, which we may suppose non-empty (the empty chain has $$I$$ as an upper bound), and let $$N = \bigcup_{\alpha \in \Delta} C_{\alpha}$$. We shall prove that $$N \in \Omega$$.

First, $$N$$ is an ideal. Note that $$N \neq \emptyset$$, since $$C_{\alpha} \subseteq N$$ for $$\alpha \in \Delta$$ and each $$C_{\alpha}$$ is non-empty. Let $$x, y \in N$$. Then there exist $$\alpha, \beta \in \Delta$$ such that $$x \in C_{\alpha}$$ and $$y \in C_{\beta}$$. Since $$(C_{\alpha})$$ is a chain, we have that $$C_{\alpha} \subseteq C_{\beta}$$ or $$C_{\beta} \subseteq C_{\alpha}$$; without loss of generality, $$C_{\alpha} \subseteq C_{\beta}$$. Then $$x, y \in C_{\beta}$$, and since $$C_{\beta}$$ is an ideal, $$x + y \in C_{\beta} \subseteq N$$. In the same way, if $$x \in N$$ and $$r \in R$$, then $$x \in C_{\alpha}$$ for some $$\alpha$$, and $$r \cdot x \in C_{\alpha} \subseteq N$$ by absorption.

Second, $$N \neq R$$: if $$N = R$$, then $$1 \in N$$, so that there exists $$\alpha$$ such that $$1 \in C_{\alpha}$$, and therefore $$C_{\alpha} = R$$, which contradicts that $$C_{\alpha} \in \Omega$$. Finally, $$I \subseteq C_{\alpha} \subseteq N$$ for any $$\alpha \in \Delta$$, so that $$N \in \Omega$$ and $$N$$ is an upper bound of the chain $$(C_{\alpha})_{\alpha \in \Delta}$$.

Hence, by Zorn's lemma, there exists a maximal element $$\mathfrak{m}$$ in $$\Omega$$. Let us see that $$\mathfrak{m}$$ is a maximal ideal of $$R$$: by construction $$\mathfrak{m} \neq R$$ and $$I \subseteq \mathfrak{m}$$. If $$J$$ is an ideal such that $$\mathfrak{m} \subseteq J$$ and $$J \neq R$$, then $$I \subseteq \mathfrak{m} \subseteq J$$, so $$J \in \Omega$$; by the maximality of $$\mathfrak{m}$$ in $$\Omega$$, we have that $$J = \mathfrak{m}$$. That is, every ideal containing $$\mathfrak{m}$$ is $$R$$ or is $$\mathfrak{m}$$, which is the definition of a maximal ideal.

### Corollary (The maximal spectrum is non-empty and detects the non-units)

If $$R \neq \{0\}$$ is a commutative ring, then $$\operatorname{mSpec}(R) \neq \emptyset$$. Moreover,

$$
\bigcup_{\mathfrak{m} \in \operatorname{mSpec}(R)} \mathfrak{m} = R \setminus R^{\times}.
$$

***Proof:*** Since $$R \neq \{0\}$$, the ideal $$(0)$$ is proper, and by Krull's theorem there exists a maximal ideal containing it; then $$\operatorname{mSpec}(R) \neq \emptyset$$.

“$$\supseteq$$”: If $$x \in R \setminus R^{\times}$$, then $$(x) \neq R$$: otherwise $$1 \in (x)$$, that is, $$1 = r x$$ for some $$r \in R$$, and $$x$$ would be a unit. By Krull, there exists a maximal $$\mathfrak{m}$$ such that $$(x) \subseteq \mathfrak{m}$$. Conclude that $$x \in \bigcup_{\mathfrak{m} \in \operatorname{mSpec}(R)} \mathfrak{m}$$.

“$$\subseteq$$”: If $$x \in \mathfrak{m}$$ with $$\mathfrak{m}$$ maximal, then $$x \notin R^{\times}$$: if $$x$$ were a unit, we would have $$1 = x^{-1} x \in \mathfrak{m}$$ by absorption, whence $$\mathfrak{m} = R$$, which contradicts $$\mathfrak{m} \neq R$$.

### Definition (Local ring)

A commutative ring $$R \neq \{0\}$$ is *local* if it possesses exactly one maximal ideal.

### Corollary (Characterisation of local rings)

If $$R \neq \{0\}$$ is a commutative ring, then $$R$$ is local if and only if $$R \setminus R^{\times}$$ is an ideal.

***Proof:*** Exercise.

### Definition (Jacobson radical)

Let $$R \neq \{0\}$$ be a commutative ring. We define the *Jacobson radical* as

$$
\operatorname{Rad}(R) = \bigcap_{\mathfrak{m} \in \operatorname{mSpec}(R)} \mathfrak{m}.
$$

### Exercise (Characterisation of the Jacobson radical)

Show that $$x \in \operatorname{Rad}(R)$$ if and only if $$1 - x y \in R^{\times}$$ for each $$y \in R$$.
